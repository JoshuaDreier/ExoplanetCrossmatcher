\subsubsection{Coordinate Matching}
\label{sec:coordinate_calibration}

The NASA exoplanet archive (NEA) is by far the most cited exoplanet catalog and thus deserves special attention among the input sources. NEA's coordinates are assembled from many different sources and the coordinate epoch can be read or estimated from the source they list for the entry. 
As explained in \cref{fig:nea_coord_cal}, using the proper-motion-aware \cref{eq:proper_motion_radius} on a set of ID-matched stars, we achieve a matching accuracy of 100\% (of the 792 ID-matched stars in the sample). In fact $r_0 = \qty{10}{''}$ is chosen as the lowest number for which this holds true. When using the simpler method of a fixed matching radius, then $r_{\min}=\qty{10}{''}$ matches 81.4\% of the sample and $r_{\text{fallback}} = \qty{50}{''}$ matches 99.4\%. So, we would expect the same efficiency for 50'' in the rows that the proper motion aware formula cannot be applied to, while keeping the FP chance low. By comparing for each planet which stars were matched, once by ID and once by coordinates, we note that none of the matching methods above introduced any FPs\footnote{assuming the ID-matches to be false-positive free}.
Exo-MerCat does not carry columns for proper motion, thus only a default search radius $r_{\text{default}}$ can be applied. Comparing \cref{fig:nea_coord_cal}'s median ${4.1''}$ crossmatching distance with \cref{fig:emc_coord_cal}'s $\qty{3.0}{mas}$, the average crossmatching distance between HPICxLIFEcat and Exo-MerCat is generally around three orders of magnitude smaller than the median crossmatching distance between HPICxLIFEcat and NEA. Due to this difference arising from the different coordinate sourcing between the two catalogs, we cannot naively transfer the search radii for the NEA method to EMC. However, $r_{\text{default}} = \qty{50}{arcsec}$ in this case is the minimal threshold such that all of the 1285 ID-matched exoplanets in EMC can be matched via coordinates, with no FPs occuring.
\subsubsection{Combined Matching}
As shown in \cref{fig:yield}, matching HPICxLIFEcat with NEA produces a total of 860 matches, 92\% of which were matched using both the coordinate and the ID method and 53 were only matched by coordinates, 15 solely by ID. Matching HPICxLIFEcat with Exo-MerCat, results in 1295 matches, with 99\% of matches being made with both methods, only 10 coordinate-only matches and none being matched just by ID.  Thus coordinate-only matching proves to be the edge case, as matching with NEA and Exo-MerCat primarily results in matches based on linked identifiers \textit{and} coordinates. Exo-MerCat matches are almost a superset of the NEA matches; the only exceptions are high-mass directly imaged planets that were removed by Exo-MerCat's Brown Dwarf-removal step. However, note that despite Exo-MerCat being a superset of NEA, the absolute number of ID- and coordinate-only matches is lower for Exo-MerCat. 
\subsection{Population Analysis}
\label{sec:population_analysis}
Exo-MerCat assembles the largest published list of exoplanets, and is thus very useful in gaining a sense of the broader known planet population. In \cref{fig:pop_distr} we can see certain features of the diagram correspond to specific parts of the exoplanet population. Note that part of the data was taken from post-Exo-MerCat enrichment computations (a version with only published parameters can be found in \cref{fig:pop_distr_nocomp} in Appendix \cref{appdx:nocomp}):
The "bulge" on the top right (high insolation, high radius) represents the hot jupiter type planets. The long thin strand pointing downwards on the right half of the plot are wide-separation planets observed via direct imaging.

Confirmed planets are drawn in the darker shade and unconfirmed candidates in the lighter shade. We can compare the overall distribution with just the distribution of planets matched by HPICxLIFEcat, here done in heatmap form to better assess how many planets share similar parameters, presented in \cref{fig:pop_heatmap} (and a decomposition into the spectral categories of the same heatmap in appendix \cref{appdx:spectral_category} \cref{fig:pop_heatmap_spectral}). Note some differences between the planetary populations populations: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item The feature of hot jupiters (upper right quadrant) is nearly absent in the HPICxLIFEcat planet population.
    \item The average radius and insolation values in the HPICxLIFEcat side are significantly lower than in the entire Exo-MerCat catalog ($\qty{4.4}{\Rearth}$ vs $\qty{7.5}{\Rearth}$ and $\qty{90}{\Searth}$ vs $\qty{558}{\Searth}$).
\end{itemize}
In contrast, the corresponding host star population mainly changes by an over-representation of late type stars\footnote{here identified with "Very-low luminosity or Low-luminosity star"} in the HPICxLIFEcat dataset.
Note further, in neither population are any planets excluded from being classified as temperate and rocky by having suitable insolation values and radii of less than $0.5\Rearth$.

Particularly interesting to the LIFE mission is the population of rocky habitable zone planets. Using the spectral categories and habitability conventions from \cref{sec:conventions}, we find the amount of habitable planets per spectral category for the general population and the crossmatched HPICxLIFEcat population in \cref{fig:rocky_temp_yield}. The full list of the 20 (resp. 83) rocky, temperate planet candidates underlying that figure is given in \cref{appdx:rocky_temp_tables}: \cref{tab:rocky_temp_table} for the distance-limited HPIC$\times$LIFEcat crossmatched sample and \cref{tab:rocky_temp_table_full} for the entire Exo-MerCat catalog.
Note that the share of habitable planets is significantly higher in the matched sample than in the general population: A total 20 habitable planets occupy 1.6\% (7 confirmed, 0.5\%)  among $\num{1216}$ in the crossmatched population; compared to 83 habitable planets (18 confirmed) among $\num{16132}$ planets in the general population giving 0.68\% (0.1\% confirmed).
